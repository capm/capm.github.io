Post-trade interoperability and settlement finality in the nuam single market

What interoperable clearing in Chile, Colombia and Peru means for legal finality, and where a tokenised security would sit

Christian Portocarrero/20 November 2026/Desk review of public legal texts

Key findings

  • The three countries use the same legal method: a transfer order is final when a recognised system accepts it, and the system's own rulebook determines the point in time at which this occurs.
  • This protection is territorial. Under MILA each trade settled in one system under one law, while under nuam's interoperable clearing one trade can pass through two central counterparties and two different legal systems.
  • The interoperability rules adopted between 2023 and 2026 are prudential. None of these rules recognises the finality of a foreign system or aligns the moments of acceptance, and Peru leaves that sequence to be determined by agreement between the CCPs.
  • The post-trade rules do not refer to distributed ledgers, so a token transfer outside a recognised system has contractual finality only. The shortest route to tokenised securities is that the depository keeps its own register on a ledger.

1. Why finality, and why now

Settlement finality is the moment in time when a transfer of securities or cash can no longer be reversed, even if one of the participants becomes insolvent. It is established by statute, and without it a liquidator could challenge the transfers made shortly before a participant's insolvency.

This issue is especially pertinent to Chile, Colombia and Peru at this time. One reason is nuam, the holding company that integrates the Santiago, Colombia and Lima stock exchanges. Its operating model relies on one common trading platform and three interoperable central counterparties (CCPs).[1] In preparation for interoperable clearing, regulators have created the framework for it to operate: Chile introduced a regime to recognise foreign CCPs through a change in the law in December 2023;[2] Colombia authorised CCPs to enter into interoperability agreements by decree in August 2026;[3] and Peru, which did not previously have a CCP, created the legal figure on 5 November 2026 and gave CAVALI six months to adapt its rulebook.[4] Another reason is tokenisation. CCAF and Fii found that there is still a lack of specificity on how custody, transfer and post-trade are handled in emerging markets, and that Chile, Colombia and Peru are in the early stages of exploring what type of policies they will implement.[5] In this note, I examine whether that finding holds up when compared with the laws of Chile, Colombia and Peru.

2. From MILA to nuam: what changes in law

MILA integrated order routing and the post-trade process remained under national jurisdiction. In accordance with articles 4.2 and 30.1 of the Peruvian regulation, clearing and settlement are subject to the rules of the country in which the security is listed and traded.[6] Cross-border holdings were processed using reciprocal omnibus accounts established between CAVALI and each foreign depository (article 26). The entry that prevails is the one made in the depository in which the final holder of the security maintains the account (article 29). CAVALI's record of foreign holdings is purely informational (article 28.4). As such, there was only one settlement system, only one point at which finality occurred, and only one official record for each transaction.

nuam gives up that simplicity to obtain something MILA did not deliver, a single pool of liquidity in which a broker trades any of the three markets from home and faces only its local CCP, without a correspondent broker in the other country. As soon as a trade is matched, it goes to the CCP of each intermediary's country, each CCP represents its members before the other two, and settlement occurs in the depository of the country where the security is registered.[1] This creates a legal relationship that MILA did not have. If a Lima-based broker buys a Santiago-listed share from a Chilean broker there are three positions (broker to Peruvian CCP, Peruvian CCP to Chilean CCP, Chilean CCP to broker), and the one in the middle is new (Figure 1).

Custody is not uniform either. The depositories of Peru and Colombia record the final beneficiary, while Chile keeps omnibus accounts, so the model uses mirror accounts to carry the beneficiary information across the link.[1]

Finality is granted to orders accepted by a system recognised by either the law or the authority supervising the system, and not to transfers in general; in Peru an arrangement that has not been recognised is excluded from this protection (articles 2 to 4).[7] The moment of acceptance is determined by the system's own rulebook, which is in line with Principle 8 of the Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures,[10] and it means that three CCPs and three depositories can each have a different time of acceptance. Protection is territorial, since each statute is an exception to the insolvency law of the country where it applies. Peru contemplated linked systems, since an order accepted in one System and settled in another keeps its effects from the first acceptance (article 6), but both have to be Systems recognised under Peruvian law.

The interoperability rules do not close this gap, and they were not designed to. A regulation or a decree cannot extend an insolvency exception that is set in a statute. They are prudential rules. Peru and Colombia require bilateral collateral between the CCPs, restrict the mutualisation of losses across the link, and require a cooperation agreement between supervisors.[4][3] Chile works differently: the law allows a CCP to sign agreements with foreign entities under criteria that the CMF may set, and Title VIII lets the CMF recognise foreign CCPs. However, I have not found equivalent conditions in the law or in the current CCLV rulebook, so they will depend on CMF rules that are still to come.[2] On default resources all three CCPs end up with the same result, cover for their two largest exposures, although in Chile and Colombia this is set in their own rulebooks.[11]

On finality, the rules of Peru and Colombia provide for each CCP to act as a counterparty of the other, so the position between the two CCPs is accepted on each side by a recognised system and protected by the relevant national law. However, there is no provision on the sequence in which the two acceptances are to occur, or on whether they will be recognised outside their respective jurisdictions. Peru requires the CCPs to agree the sequence, Colombia does not refer to the issue, and Chile applies the provisions on notification of court orders to recognised foreign CCPs, but does not explicitly apply the finality rule. The risk lies in the interim period. If an insolvency order is issued against a clearing member after its home CCP has accepted the trade and before the foreign CCP has accepted the corresponding position, two courts would apply two statutes, and the agreement entered into by the two CCPs would not bind a liquidator in the other jurisdiction. Principles 1 and 20 require a legal basis that is enforceable in all relevant jurisdictions, and require linked infrastructures to identify the legal risk associated with the link.[10] The Banco Central de Reserva del Perú and the Banco de la República, in their 2026 reports, both include legal risk among the conditions for interoperability.[12][13]

4. Where a tokenised security would sit

I found no reference to distributed ledgers or tokens in the post-trade rules and infrastructure rulebooks of the three countries. The only recognition is on the cash side. Chile's Fintec Law treats representations of money recorded on distributed ledgers as payment orders and means of payment, provided they meet the standards set by the Central Bank.[14]

Read together with the previous section, this means that a transfer of securities recorded on a ledger outside a recognised system is not covered by the finality statute. The transfer may have become final within the network (the ledger was updated and the network members consider the record as settled), and still be open to challenge by a liquidator, because no law makes it irrevocable. On some public networks even this first type of finality is a matter of probability for a period of time.[5][15] Atomic delivery versus payment eliminates the time gap between the two sides of the transaction; however, it does not determine which law protects either side against reversal.

Other jurisdictions have accepted weaker protection in exchange for learning. For example, the European Union allows a depository to run a DLT settlement system without designation under the Settlement Finality Directive if it adopts compensatory measures against insolvency risk, with a cap of EUR 6 billion per infrastructure.[16] The UK sandbox does not require designation either; each operator has to define and disclose its moments of entry and irrevocability, and linked systems must adopt identical moments.[17] The European Commission has since proposed converting the Settlement Finality Directive into a Regulation, in part because the national rules had diverged.[18] The nuam regulators can still prevent that kind of divergence, as the three statutes share a design and interoperable clearing is not live yet.

Box 1

From book entry to token

In the three countries a dematerialised security is its entry in the depository's register. In Peru the register of an issue belongs to a single institution and transfer operates by book entry (Ley del Mercado de Valores, articles 212, 213 and 215),[19] in Colombia the book entry kept by a central depository is constitutive of the right (Ley 964, article 12),[9] and in Chile transfers are made by debit and credit in the depository's accounts (Ley 18.876, article 7).[20]

A token issued by a platform over securities held in the depository would be in the same position as MILA's mirror record, it gives information and no title. Another option is that the depository keeps its own register on a ledger. I could not find a rule that prescribes the technology: Chile allows the register of a dematerialised issue to be kept "by any means" that prevents tampering,[20] Colombia sets principles such as priority and an unbroken chain of entries,[21] and Peru sets outcomes such as security, back-ups and the ability to reconstruct every entry.[19] In that case only the rulebook changes, the ledger stays inside a recognised system and the finality statute applies. In Peru the ledger would have to be operated by the depository alone, because the register of an issue is attributed to a single institution and cannot be shared with other operators. Peru's criterion for acceptance, that funds and securities are verified as available,[8] would coincide with settlement itself in an atomic transaction. This is my reading and each supervisor would have to confirm it. Tokens issued natively, without a depository, would need legislation.[22]

5. Policy considerations

I order the options by the legal effort they require. The first three do not need any change in the law.

  1. Require the finality sequence as a condition of approval. The SMV approves interoperability agreements in advance; the SFC receives these agreements before they start and may ask for changes; and the CMF has the authority to set criteria for agreements with foreign entities. The three supervisors could require a common sequence of acceptance moments for the CCPs and depositories involved in an agreement, defined in the same terms in each rulebook.
  2. Set up a supervisory college. The three securities supervisors and the three central banks could oversee the link jointly, in line with Responsibility E,[10] and commission legal opinions on whether the courts of each country would respect a transfer accepted by a linked foreign system. Although a legal opinion is not law, it would identify the risks involved and tell the legislators what changes need to be made.
  3. Require disclosure. Each infrastructure would disclose its finality rules for interoperable trades, much like the UK requires of its sandbox operators.
  4. Test a tokenised leg inside the perimeter. Colombia's regulation includes a controlled testing space, and in 2021 its supervisor authorised a blockchain bond pilot.[21][5] The Peruvian securities regulator has the evaluation of a regulatory sandbox on its agenda for 2027. A joint test with value limits would give evidence on finality without changing the law.
  5. Legislate mutual recognition of finality. Each statute would protect the orders accepted by a recognised foreign system. Chile's Title VIII provides the conditions, while Peru's rule on linked systems provides the effect.

The first three options should be implemented before interoperable clearing goes live.

6. Scope and limits

This document is a review of public legal texts and official reports published up to 12 November 2026. It is not intended to provide legal advice. Because CAVALI's revised rulebook and the agreements between the CCPs are not yet publicly available, I reviewed the general framework, as opposed to the specific details of the final arrangements.
